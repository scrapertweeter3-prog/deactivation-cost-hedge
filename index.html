The $30 a week that hedges a $5,280 freeze

A gig account is a single point of failure the worker does not control. One flag review and the payouts stop, and the deactivation guides that price the damage put the freeze near six weeks. For a driver on one app, forty hours a week at $22 gross, the week banks $880 and one freeze burns $5,280. The failure is not the deactivation. It is that every dollar of income sat behind one login.

The fix is a stack, and the stack has a sticker. Thirty hours stay on the primary at $22 and ten hours a week keep a smaller app warm at $19. Weekly gross drops from $880 to $850. That $30, about 3.4 percent of the week, is the premium, and it gets paid whether or not the freeze ever comes.

When the flag lands, the stack changes what the freeze costs. The frozen primary had been carrying $660 of the week; the warm app keeps $190 arriving. Six weeks of freeze still brings in $1,140 that the solo account never sees, so the burn lands at $4,140 instead of $5,280 and each freeze event rebates $1,140. The premium buys its payout in the week the account dies, which is the only week insurance ever pays out on.

The break-even is countable. A $1,140 rebate against a $30 weekly premium means the stack pays for itself at one freeze every 38 weeks. One event a year leaves the stack $420 behind: $1,560 of premium against $1,140 of rebate. Two events in a year flips it, because the premium is still $1,560 while the rebates total $2,280, and the stack finishes $720 ahead. Any stretch between freezes shorter than 38 weeks and the second login is carrying its weight.

The ledger inverts the usual complaint that running two apps splits the hustle. The second app is not a distraction from the main income, it is the hedge on it. An account that pays all week and can die on a Friday is a position, not a paycheck, and positions get hedged.
